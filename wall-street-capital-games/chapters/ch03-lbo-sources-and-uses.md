# Chapter 3: LBO Sources, Uses, and Value Creation

**Primary source:** *Barbarians at the Gate* by Bryan Burrough and John Helyar

## Core Idea

A leveraged buyout converts a company's future cash flow into present acquisition capacity. The sponsor contributes a fraction of the purchase price, lenders finance the rest, and control enables operational, financial, and governance changes.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Sources-and-uses bridge**
  - When to use: Evaluate any control acquisition.
  - How: List purchase equity value, refinanced debt, fees, and cash as uses; list debt tranches, sponsor equity, rollover equity, and other financing as sources.
- **LBO return decomposition**
  - When to use: Test what must go right for sponsor returns.
  - How: Separate EBITDA growth, margin change, debt paydown, free-cash-flow conversion, asset sales, and entry-to-exit multiple change.
- **Debt-capacity constraint**
  - When to use: A bidder raises price by adding leverage.
  - How: Stress interest coverage, mandatory amortization, maturities, covenant headroom, cyclicality, and refinancing access.

## Key Concepts

- **Sponsor equity:** Risk capital contributed by the private-equity buyer.
- **Acquisition debt:** Borrowing used to fund the purchase and typically serviced by the target group.
- **Management rollover:** Existing owners reinvest part of their equity into the new structure.
- **Exit multiple:** Enterprise-value valuation used when the sponsor sells or lists the company.
- **Free cash flow:** Cash available after operating needs and investment to service debt or distribute.

## Mental Models

- More leverage increases equity sensitivity; it does not create operating value by itself.
- Distinguish value creation from value transfer among shareholders, creditors, employees, and tax authorities.
- Reverse-engineer the underwriting case from the required equity return.

## Anti-patterns

- **Debt paydown as free alpha:** Debt can only be repaid if the business produces cash after investment needs.
- **Multiple expansion as base case:** Paying a high price and assuming a higher exit valuation hides weak economics.
- **Ignoring fees:** Advisory, financing, transaction, and monitoring fees can materially change returns.

## Worked Example

For a hypothetical $10 billion enterprise purchase, build uses for equity purchase, debt refinancing, and fees. Fund them with senior debt, junior debt, and sponsor equity. Forecast operating cash flow, subtract interest and reinvestment, and calculate remaining debt at exit. Recalculate returns with flat EBITDA and a lower exit multiple to identify whether the thesis survives without favorable markets.

## Key Takeaways

1. Build sources and uses before discussing strategic narratives.
2. Attribute returns to operations, deleveraging, and valuation separately.
3. Stress refinancing and downturn cases, not only average cash flow.
4. Control has value only if it enables changes that outweigh price and financing risk.
