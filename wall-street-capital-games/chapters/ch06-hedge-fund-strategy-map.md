# Chapter 6: Hedge-Fund Strategy Map

**Primary source:** *More Money Than God* by Sebastian Mallaby

## Core Idea

"Hedge fund" describes an organizational and incentive form, not one investment method. Compare funds by source of edge, market exposure, horizon, liquidity, capacity, leverage, and failure mode.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Strategy fingerprint**
  - When to use: Classify a fund or proposed strategy.
  - How: Record edge, universe, gross/net exposure, liquidity, turnover, leverage, catalyst, capacity, and principal loss mode.
- **Alpha-versus-beta decomposition**
  - When to use: Returns look exceptional.
  - How: Test whether performance is compensation for market direction, illiquidity, leverage, short volatility, factor exposure, or repeatable selection.
- **Organizational fit test**
  - When to use: A strategy is moved into a different fund structure.
  - How: Match investor lockups, redemption terms, financing, team incentives, and governance to the assets' liquidity and horizon.

## Key Concepts

- **Global macro:** Positions based on currencies, rates, commodities, and policy regimes.
- **Long-short equity:** Long and short positions intended to isolate selection while controlling some market exposure.
- **Event driven:** Positions whose payoff depends on corporate events.
- **Relative-value arbitrage:** Trades price relationships while hedging broad direction.
- **Activist investing:** Builds influence to change corporate policy, capital allocation, or control.

## Mental Models

- Compare strategies by their hidden short option: the condition under which losses accelerate.
- Treat liquidity terms as part of the investment strategy.
- A high Sharpe ratio can be produced by smoothed or rare downside.

## Anti-patterns

- **One hedge-fund benchmark:** Strategies with different risks should not be compared only by headline return.
- **Fee-adjusted complacency:** Strong gross performance may not translate to investor outcomes after fees and taxes.
- **Scalable forever:** Successful edges attract capital and can erase themselves.

## Worked Example

Compare a macro fund, a market-neutral equity fund, and a merger-arbitrage fund. The macro fund may tolerate liquid daily risk but suffer policy regime errors. The equity fund needs borrow, data, and factor control. The merger fund earns a spread but carries deal-break downside. The same return target does not imply the same leverage, redemption terms, or portfolio role.

## Key Takeaways

1. Identify the actual return engine before evaluating performance.
2. Match funding and investor liquidity to asset liquidity.
3. Measure factor and tail exposures, not only volatility.
4. Capacity and crowding are endogenous limits to alpha.
